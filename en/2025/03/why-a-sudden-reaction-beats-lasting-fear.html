Why a sudden reaction is better than lasting fear

2025-03-28

Why a sudden reaction is better than lasting fear

We've all heard the saying: “Better safe than sorry.” Behind it sits the idea that it's wiser to live in constant caution than to be caught out by a sudden scare. But what if I told you that sometimes the opposite is true? What if a sudden, instinctive fright holds a kind of strength that lasting, paralysing fear lacks?

Think about it. Fear is often a shadow that stays with us, lurking somewhere at the back of our mind or turning our everyday life grey. We worry about the future, we're afraid of failure, of rejection, of illness. This constant state of alert is exhausting and stressful, and it often stops us from acting, from actually living.

Then there's the scare. That sudden tightening in your chest when someone honks at you out of nowhere in the street, when you jump at a loud noise, or when you're suddenly faced with a potential danger. It's an instant reaction that triggers our fight-or-flight instinct. A scare leaves no time for weighing things up or for anxiety: it drives you straight into action.

The scare as an instinctive compass

For our ancestors, being startled was life-saving. A sudden noise in the forest triggered an instant reaction that could help them avoid a predator's attack. We rarely have to run from lions today, but the instinct is still in us. When we get a fright, the body gets ready to act: adrenaline surges, the heart beats faster, the muscles tense. And that energy mobilises us.

Often it's a scare that jolts us out of passivity. Maybe we keep putting off an important task because we're afraid we won't be good enough. But a sudden jolt of alarm as a deadline approaches can force us to finally get started. Or maybe we dread a confrontation, but in a tense moment the anger that comes from a sudden fright helps us stand up for ourselves.

The power of action against the paralysis of fear

Fear often paralyses. It freezes us, makes us unsure, and stops us from doing anything at all. A scare, on the other hand, pushes us to act. Even if that action isn't the most considered one, at least it gets something moving. And very often it's exactly that momentum that carries us over the difficulties.

Of course, an excessive or unfounded scare can do harm too. But if fear has taken over your life for good, it may be worth asking whether a little “fright” now and then wouldn't be a more rousing, more mobilising force.

The scare as a wake-up call

Sometimes a sudden fright shines a light on something we've been ignoring. A near miss on the road might make us drive more carefully. A worrying test result at the doctor's might make us realise we need to look after our health better. These scares aren't pleasant, but they can be the catalyst for positive change.

The goal is not fear, but preparedness

I want to be clear: I'm not saying we should go looking for scares. The point is that instead of living in lasting fear, we can sometimes find a strength in the sudden reaction that helps us act and face the challenge in front of us.

Best of all, of course, is not needing either. Preparedness, as we discussed with the emergency kit, is the key. If we know we're ready for the unexpected, it reduces fear, and perhaps even softens the shock when something does happen.

Think it over

When did you last feel fear paralyse you? And when did a sudden scare help you act? It's worth considering that sometimes a sudden reaction isn't so bad after all. In fact, it may be exactly what we need to step out of our comfort zone and face the challenge.
