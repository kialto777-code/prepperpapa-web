72 hours without power at 40°C: are you really ready for this summer?

2026-07-26

Two in the afternoon. The thermometer reads 41°C in the shade. Then the power goes. Not for five minutes, not for an hour. The air conditioning stops, the fridge starts to warm up, your phone is on 34 percent, and sooner or later the tap gives you nothing but air, because the pump that keeps the water pressure up runs on electricity too.

This is not a scene from a disaster film. It happened this summer, in more than one place in Central Europe.

What actually happened this summer

In the second half of June, a heat alert covered an entire Central European country, and at the end of the month it was raised to the highest level. On 30 June that country's all-time heat record fell at 42°C. A long heatwave doesn't just make you sweat, it strains the infrastructure too. Official reports from several towns told of water supply problems, some from burst pipes, some from failed pumps, and the heat disrupted rail traffic as well.

Then comes the stormy side: heatwaves usually end with a cold front and a line of thunderstorms, bringing hail, gale-force winds and cloudbursts. Fallen trees and torn-down power lines are what cause outages lasting several days, typically in smaller villages where the repairs have to wait their turn.

In April 2025, a grid collapse plunged most of the Iberian Peninsula into darkness for hours. The real lesson was not what happened technically. It was that millions of people realised at the same moment that they didn't have a single box of matches at home.

Why 72 hours?

Since 2025, the European Union has recommended that every household keep enough supplies to look after itself for at least 72 hours. Not a bunker, not ten years of tinned food. Three days.

Why that number? Because crisis management experience says that after a major event, it typically takes between 24 and 72 hours for organised help to reach you. Utilities come back, shops reopen, rescue teams get through. In those first three days, though, you are your own rescue service.

That's the good news in it. 72 hours isn't paranoia, it's a job you can get done in a weekend, on a realistic budget.

Water: this runs out first

In the heat, an adult needs around 3 litres of water a day, and that's just for drinking. Not for washing, not for flushing the toilet, not for cooking.

Work out your own figure:

  • 3 litres of drinking water per person per day x 3 days x the number of people in your home. For a family of four, that's 36 litres just to drink.
  • Plus at least 5 litres per person per day for hygiene and the toilet, if you have the space.
  • Count the dog and the cat too. They're the easiest to forget.

What you can do today: buy 5-litre bottles, put them at the bottom of the larder in a cool, dark place, and replace them every six months. Five minutes and very little money.

Worth even more: keep a bathtub water storage bag or a clean 20-30 litre plastic container at home. If water restrictions are announced in advance, there is still pressure in the system. Whoever fills up then will have water. Whoever waits until it runs out ends up queuing at the water tanker.

If you're not sure where the water came from: boil it for one minute, or use a microfilter. Chlorine disinfection tablets are cheap and don't go off, so keep some in your stock.

Keeping cool without power at 40°C

A home without air conditioning in summer is far more dangerous than one without heating in winter. Heatstroke comes on fast, and it can floor an elderly family member or someone with a heart condition within a few hours.

What you can do:

  • Close the place up during the day. Shutters, blackout curtains, windows shut. Keeping the heat out is easier than driving it out.
  • Air the rooms at dawn and late in the evening, with a cross-draught.
  • A wet towel on your neck, wrists and ankles. That's where the big blood vessels run, so that's where you shed heat fastest.
  • A simple battery or USB fan and a big power bank will do more for your comfort than anything else on this list.
  • Move downstairs. If you have a ground floor, a cellar or an outdoor summer kitchen, sleep there. Heat rises.

One item that surprises a lot of people: some of your medicines are heat-sensitive. Insulin, some eye drops and antibiotics degrade above 25-30°C. If the fridge stops, these are the first things to go into the cool bag, not the cold cuts.

Food that won't spoil in a heatwave

A closed fridge stays cold for roughly four hours. A full freezer can hold for up to 48 hours; if it's half full, count on half that. The rule is simple: don't open it. Every time you open the door, you lose hours.

On day one, eat from the fridge; on day two, from the freezer; on day three, from the larder. In that order, not the other way round.

Keep three days' worth of food in your larder that needs no cooking and no chilling:

  • tinned fish, meat, beans, sweetcorn, ready-made vegetable stew
  • rusks, crackers, cereal bars
  • peanut butter, honey, dried fruit, nuts and seeds
  • a bag of salt. In the heat you lose salt through sweat, and if you then drink nothing but water, you'll feel even worse.

A camping stove with a gas canister is the backup to your backup. But at 40°C, the smart move is to cook little, not a lot.

Power: only for what matters

You don't need a generator. In most households there are three things that really have to keep working: the phone, a light and the radio.

  • A 20,000 mAh power bank charges your phone four or five times. Two of them cover three days.
  • A head torch, not candles. A candle is an open flame, and with children and a dog in the house that's a bad idea.
  • A battery or wind-up radio. If the mobile network goes down too, this is your only channel to the outside world.
  • A car charger. Your car is a complete backup power source, you just haven't thought of it that way until now.

You can add a solar charger, but it shouldn't be the foundation. The foundation is a charged power bank, tonight.

Cash, paper, contact

Card terminals and cash machines are dead without power. So is online banking if there's no mobile data. Keep enough cash at home, in small notes, to cover food and fuel for three days.

Print out or write down on paper:

  • your family members' phone numbers, because you don't know a single one by heart
  • your doctor's details, and the names and doses of your medicines
  • a meeting point in case you can't reach each other

Agree in advance who goes where if the phones are down. This is the piece most often missing: the family has the physical supplies, but not the plan.

The 20-minute test

Don't take it for granted that you're prepared. Test it.

Tonight, switch off the main breaker for 20 minutes. That's enough to find out:

  1. Can you find the head torch, and does it have batteries in it?
  2. Is the power bank charged, or has it been lying empty in a drawer for six months?
  3. Can you get a drink of water without turning on the tap?
  4. Does the whole family know where the supplies are, or only you?

What those 20 minutes show you is worth more than any list. And whatever turns out to be missing, you get at the weekend.

The bottom line

You're not preparing for the apocalypse. You're preparing for a three-day power cut in the middle of summer, which this year was reality in more than one town in Central Europe. The whole thing takes a weekend and a modest budget, and from then on a blackout in a heatwave is not a disaster, just an inconvenience.

That's the difference between panic and preparedness.
